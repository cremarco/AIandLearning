### Script — words to say

Self-direction and self-regulation are related, but they are not the same.

Self-direction includes choosing learning needs, goals, and resources. Self-regulation means planning, checking progress, and reflecting during learning. This can happen in a task set by a teacher.

For example, I may plan and check my reading of an assigned article. In a self-directed project, I also choose the question and the sources.

Being independent does not mean working alone. Teachers, peers, and access support can still help.

### Script — words to say

Here is a simple way to make learning decisions visible.

Before a task, plan: what am I trying to understand? During the task, monitor: what is working, and what needs checking? After the task, reflect: what changed, and what will I try next?

These questions can bring us back to an earlier step. We can repeat this cycle and return to earlier steps.

The diagram is a simple teaching aid based on learning models. It is not a psychological test.

8/34 — SLIDE S08 — Four different outcomes
Page 9 / 44
05:30–07:00; 1.5 min

### Presenter actions — do not read aloud

- Point to one card for each outcome, in the order of the script.
- Pause briefly after the question about a new task.

### Script — words to say

Four outcomes help us read the evidence carefully.

First, the assisted product: how good is the answer while help is available? Second, an independent check: what can the learner explain or do when the tool is not supplying the answer? Third, retention: what remains after some time? Fourth, transfer: can the learner use the idea in a new task?

These questions need different checks. An immediate test cannot by itself show long-term retention. Feeling confident or enjoying the activity is also different from demonstrating learning.

Independent content checks still keep screen readers, dictation, translation, and other access support. We are checking the target skill, not the person’s ability to work without accommodations.

### Script — words to say

Fan and colleagues ran a writing experiment with 117 university students. The students spoke Chinese as their first language and English as a second language.

There were four conditions: ChatGPT support, a human expert, learning analytics, and no support.

ChatGPT improved the essays. But the study did not find a significant advantage in knowledge or transfer.

A better essay can therefore be a better product without clear evidence of better learning.

This was one laboratory task with small groups. The phrase ‘metacognitive laziness’ is the authors’ interpretation. It is not a diagnosis or evidence of lasting cognitive harm.

10/34 — SLIDE S09 — Better practice can hide a weaker independent test
Page 10 / 44
09:00–10:30; 1.5 min

### Presenter actions — do not read aloud

- In paragraph two, point first to +48%, then to −17%.
- Pause after the sentence about relative changes.
- Pause briefly before the final paragraph.

### Script — words to say

Bastani and colleagues studied about one thousand pupils in one Turkish school over four sessions.

With unrestricted AI, called GPT Base, practice scores were 48 percent higher than the control group. But on an immediate exam without AI help, scores were 17 percent lower.

These are relative changes compared with the control group, not percentage-point differences.

With a more guided version, GPT Tutor, practice scores were 127 percent higher than the control group. It avoided the observed exam loss, but did not show a significant exam advantage.

The key distinction is between performing with support and performing after that support ends. Better practice did not automatically become better independent performance.

This was one mathematics setting. It did not test long-term retention, and it does not show that every AI tool harms learning.

### Script — words to say

Kestin and colleagues studied 194 Harvard students in two introductory physics lessons.

Students using a specially designed AI tutor scored higher on immediate tests than those in active classroom lessons. The study used a randomized crossover design: students experienced both types of lesson.

This is a useful positive result. But the comparison included different teaching arrangements, feedback, and individual pacing. It did not isolate one general property of the AI model.

The tests were immediate. There was no delayed retention test. The 316 reported observations were not 316 different people.

So we can say that this designed tutor worked better on the immediate tests in this specific comparison. We cannot turn that result into a promise for every learner or subject.
