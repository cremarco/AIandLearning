EDU FORUM 2026 — ACTIVITY PACKET
English participant materials | Prepared 31 October 2026

These are original workshop activities informed by the shared research dossier.
They are not a validated assessment scale or an already tested integrated method.
No account, app, upload or internet connection is required during the activities.
The evidence cards below are short paraphrases of checked sources, not raw abstracts.

ACCESS AND PARTICIPATION
Write, speak, dictate or use a structured map. Work alone or with a partner.
Keep screen readers, text-to-speech, dictation, translation and approved
accommodations throughout. Independent reasoning means making and explaining
your own learning decisions. It does not mean giving up access support.
You do not have to disclose a diagnosis or share a personal experience publicly.
Timeboxes can be extended; speed and fluent English are not assessment criteria.

EVIDENCE CARD A01 — MATHEMATICS
Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school
mathematics. Bastani et al. (2025), PNAS.
Design/context: a classroom-randomized mathematics study.
Finding: unrestricted GPT-4 improved assisted practice but reduced performance
on an immediate unassisted exam. Guardrailed tutoring avoided the observed exam
loss without establishing an independent-learning gain.
Limit: distinguish assisted practice from an immediate independent exam;
neither measures durable learning in every subject. Reported percentages are
relative changes, not percentage points.
Source: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122

EVIDENCE CARD A02 — UNIVERSITY PHYSICS
AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: an RCT introducing a novel
research-based design in an authentic educational setting.
Kestin et al. (2025), Scientific Reports.
Design/context: an RCT with 194 Harvard students compared a designed AI tutor
with active-classroom physics lessons.
Finding: immediate posttests favored tutoring.
Limit: this compared instructional packages. The 316 posttest observations do
not represent 316 participants. Long-term retention was not established.
Source: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6

EVIDENCE CARD A03 — WRITING
Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence
on learning motivation, processes, and performance.
Fan et al. (2025), British Journal of Educational Technology.
Design/context: a randomized writing study with 117 university students.
Finding: ChatGPT-assisted essays were better, without significant advantages
in knowledge, transfer or intrinsic motivation. Self-regulation processes differed.
Limit: one laboratory task and small groups limit generalization.
“Metacognitive laziness” is an interpretation, not a diagnosis.
Source: https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544

EVIDENCE CARD A06 — SCHOOL SELF-REGULATION
Enhancing School Students’ Self-Regulated Learning through Generative AI Support:
A Randomized Controlled Trial. Fütterer et al. (2026), Educational Psychology Review.
Design/context: a preregistered RCT with 371 school students compared two
prompting approaches with standard ChatGPT across six lessons.
Finding: no significant advantage in the main outcomes.
Limit: the comparison condition also used AI. This does not show that AI equals
no AI. Knowledge assessment was brief. A nonsignificant result does not establish
universal equivalence.
Source: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8

OPTIONAL SOURCE-TYPE COMPARISON
A05 — Survey, not a student-learning experiment.
Lee et al. (2025) collected knowledge workers’ reports of critical thinking
during workplace tasks. Associations between confidence and reported effort
do not establish a causal effect on students’ learning.
Source: https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778

R03 — Institutional guidance, not an efficacy trial.
The European Commission (2026) offers principles and questions concerning
responsibility, fairness, transparency, well-being and data protection in
educational AI. Guidance can inform a design decision; it is not experimental
proof that an activity improves learning.
Source: https://doi.org/10.2766/7967834

ACTIVITY 2 — REPAIR THE ARGUMENT MAP
Indicative time: 14 minutes extended / 8 minutes short.
Materials: this text and, if useful, the evidence cards above.

The deliberately flawed argument:
AI gives fast answers → students understand → students become independent.

Equivalent structured text:
Claim 1: AI gives fast answers.
Proposed link: therefore, students understand.
Proposed link: therefore, students become independent.
Question: Which links are supported, and under what conditions?

Your task:
Label one unsupported link. Add one condition, one piece of evidence and one
learning check. Use labelled arrows or a structured list. Keep the reasoning
task; preserve access support. A polished diagram is not required.

Link we question:
________________________________________________________________________
Condition needed:
________________________________________________________________________
Evidence and its limit (name a card):
________________________________________________________________________
What the learner will do to check understanding:
________________________________________________________________________
Access support that remains available:
________________________________________________________________________

Possible relation labels:
may support / requires / does not establish / can be checked by

Debrief:
Which arrow changed, and why?
What can the structured list communicate as well as the diagram?
Which barrier could we remove without removing the reasoning task?

ACTIVITY 3 — DESIGN A LEARNING PATH, THEN TEST THE DECISION
Indicative time: 18 minutes extended / 14 minutes short.
Materials: this worksheet, evidence cards above and one coach question below.

Scenario:
A school or university is considering an AI assistant for independent study.
Design a 10-minute learning activity in which a learner evaluates this claim:
“A better AI-assisted answer proves that the learner understands more.”
Use the evidence cards already provided. Decide what help is allowed and what
the learner must explain. No live chatbot is necessary: a partner can be the coach.

Goal — The learner will be able to:
________________________________________________________________________
First attempt — Before receiving an answer, the learner will:
________________________________________________________________________
Support — A peer or AI may help with:
________________________________________________________________________
Decision and source — The learner will decide … using:
________________________________________________________________________
Independent check — The learner will explain or apply:
________________________________________________________________________
Access support retained during that check:
________________________________________________________________________

1. Make a first attempt using the access support you need.
2. Design one small activity, not a whole course.
3. Ask a partner to read the coach question. Respond without handing over
   the decision. You can also read the question yourself.
4. Try the independent check below with a partner or privately.
5. Revise one decision and record why it changed.

Coach question:
“Which part of this activity must the learner decide, and what observation
would show that they can do it?”

Prepared workshop example — not a live AI response:
“You plan to ask the tutor for a summary. What will the learner do that lets
you check whether they can judge the evidence in that summary?”

Try the check:
“Students say the tutor feels helpful. Does this establish that they will
remember more next month? Explain what is missing and propose a check.”

Pause assistance that supplies the content answer. Retain screen readers,
text-to-speech, dictation, translation, accessible materials and approved
accommodations. If the goal includes source-based reasoning, the source card
can remain available. A closed-book recall test is not the only possible
measure of independent reasoning.

One decision I revised, and why:
________________________________________________________________________

Debrief:
What did the support help you do? Which decision stayed with the learner?
What changed after the check? What would you need to observe later?

OPTIONAL FULL LEARNING-PATH FIELDS
Goal / Starting point / Success evidence / First attempt / Steps / Help allowed
and decisions retained / Sources / Uncertainty / Independent check with access
support retained / Reflection and next step.
