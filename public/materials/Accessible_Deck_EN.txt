SLIDE S52 — Andrea’s learning task
Page 11 / 88
To understand the chapter and show what he has learned, Andrea must:
1. Read
Study the chapter. Identify the key ideas and what is still unclear.
2. Represent
Build a diagram with labelled links between the concepts.
3. Check
Compare the diagram and AI suggestions with the chapter. Correct errors.
4. Explain
Use his own words to explain a new example, without AI help.
Evidence: a checked diagram, an independent explanation and a justified correction.

SLIDE S52SDL — Self-directed learning: making choices
Page 12 / 88
Andrea takes an active role: he identifies his learning needs, chooses a path and reviews his progress.
1. Identify a goal
What do I need to learn, and what do I want to achieve?
2. Choose a path
Which resources, people and strategies will help me learn?
3. Review progress
What can I explain now, and what needs more work? Self-assessment helps here.
Andrea’s example: “I need to distinguish training from inference. I will use the chapter and a concept map, then check whether I can explain the difference.”
Andrea takes responsibility for his choices, with support from teachers and peers.
[EDU_SELF_DIRECTED] National Library of Medicine. (n.d.). Self-Directed Learning as Topic (MeSH D000073819). Definition attributed to Knowles (1975), Self-directed learning: A guide for learners and teachers. New York: Association Press.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/2023167
Conceptual definition, not an efficacy trial. Knowles’s definition was verified through the NLM entry; the full original book was not consulted. The three groups and Andrea’s example are workshop illustrations.

SLIDE S52SA — Self-assessment: checking progress
Page 13 / 88
Andrea judges his progress against clear criteria, then uses that judgement to improve his work.
1. Check the criteria
What should I be able to explain or do?
2. Look for evidence
What can I demonstrate? Where do I still struggle?
3. Choose a next step
What will I revise, practise or check again?
Andrea’s example: “I can explain what a token is, but I still confuse training and inference. I will revise that link in my map and explain it again.”
AI can offer feedback; the learner checks the evidence and decides what to improve.
[EDU_SELF_ASSESSMENT] Andrade, H. L. (2019). A critical review of research on student self-assessment. Frontiers in Education, 4, 87.
https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2019.00087
Definition and review of 76 empirical studies; not validation of this workshop, its three-step illustration or AI feedback. Andrea’s example is a constructed teaching scenario.

SLIDE S52PHASES — A study process in six phases
Page 14 / 88
1. PLAN
Set your goal, identify your starting point and organise tasks and time.
2. UNDERSTAND
Clarify difficult ideas through explanations, examples and questions.
3. CONNECT
Explain ideas in your own words and organise relationships between concepts.
4. RECALL
Retrieve what you know without looking, then revisit it over time.
5. APPLY
Use what you have learned to solve problems and handle new situations.
6. CHECK
Compare your performance with criteria, identify gaps and decide what to study next.
Checking guides your next step. SUPPORT marks setup, sources and sharing.

Welcome to today's explainer. We are jumping right into what is easily one of the most fascinating, and honestly most misunderstood, topics in tech right now. How generative AI systems actually process and retain information. Look, we all interact with these tools every single day, but there's a ton of confusion about what's actually happening under the hood. Does the AI learn from every conversation we have with it? Does it secretly keep a giant memory bank of facts? Well, today we're going beyond the prompt to decode the reality of how AI really learns. Here's our roadmap for the explainer today. First, models versus complete applications. Second, training updates the parameters. Third, inference with fixed models. Fourth, retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, and finally, reliability and limitations. Okay, let's kick things off with a question that gets right to the heart of a massive misconception. If you type in this chapter, X means Y into a prompt. Does the model permanently learn that fact? Does it update its internal weights? Take a second to really think about your answer. You know, a lot of people just assume that because the AI uses that new info in its very next reply, its brain must have been permanently rewired. But as we're going to see, the reality of how these systems hold on to information is totally different from human memory. Before we get into the heavy mechanics, we need to establish a baseline vocabulary. What exactly is the engine driving all of this? At its core, a generative language model is an auto-regressive system. Now, all that fancy term means is that it assigns probabilities to token sequences. It looks at the context it already has and calculates which token, well, which word, part of a word, or even punctuation mark, might logically come next. That is literally it. Repeating that specific calculation over and over again is what builds out a response. It's a probability engine, folks. Not a conscious fact-checker. Models versus complete applications. So there is a hard, absolute boundary we have to draw between the core model itself and the software wrapped around it. The core model simply calculates token probabilities based on context. That's the engine. But the complete application, like the slick chat interfaces we use daily, that adds system instructions, web search capabilities, file retrieval, filters, and managed memory. The fluent human-sounding writing? That comes from the core model. But pulling up a PDF or browsing the web, that entirely belongs to the surrounding application. Training updates the parameters. This definitive line is absolutely crucial when we look at different ways we intervene with the AI. If you want to permanently change the model's core numerical values, you have to actually train or fine-tune it. Just adding a new message in the context window or uploading an external document only changes the information available for that one specific calculation. It does not, in any way, update the permanent weights of the model. So what does actual training look like? It's a highly mechanical, computationally heavy flow. First, massive amounts of text examples are fed into the system. The system tries to predict the next tokens, compares them to the actual text, and calculates its prediction error. Next, these intense optimization algorithms execute physical numerical updates to the parameter values to reduce that aggregate error. The end result is a permanently changed model. The text just supplies the learning signal. The mathematical optimization does the actual rewiring. It's serious heavy lifting, and it's completely separate from you just typing a prompt into a chat box. Inference with fixed models. Now contrast all that heavy lifting with ordinary inference. When you send a prompt, the model is completely frozen. Its parameters do not change. It takes your input, runs calculations based purely on that prompt and its frozen parameters, and creates a probability distribution for the next token. Finally, an output is selected. Keeping the model fixed while changing your input is really just exploring the effective context. You aren't teaching the AI anything permanent. You're just giving it different starting conditions for its math. Let's actually ground this math in reality with a recorded run from a real model, Qwen 2.5. Given the context, the capital of Italy is. The model calculates continuation probabilities. As you can see, the token for Rome gets the highest probability at 44.86%. Located gets 10.35% and so on. Notice that these values are continuation probabilities, not absolute truth scores. The model doesn't know Rome is the capital in the way you or I do. It merely calculated that Rome is the most probable next token based entirely on its fixed parameters and the context you gave it. Retrieval Augmented Generation, or RAG. So if a fixed model can't learn new facts on the fly, how in the world do we get it to answer questions about a brand new document, or today's news? Well, we use RAG. Think of it as an open book test for the AI. You ask a question, and a separate search component goes out and retrieves relevant passages from an external database, like a PDF you just uploaded. Those passages are stuffed right into the context window alongside your question and handed over to the fixed model to read. The model then generates an answer based purely on that newly provided temporary context. Let's make this crystal clear. RAG searches external documents and makes those passages available to the generation context. Crucially, it does not change the model's parameters or weights. Uploading a PDF just updates the available information for that one conversation, not the AI's permanent brain. And remember the limits here. The retrieval part can fail. It can grab completely irrelevant data. Or the model might just misinterpret the material you supplied it entirely. Reliability and Limitations. An 80% probability for a token absolutely does not mean the claim has an 80% chance of being factually true. I want to leave you with this final really important thought from our source material. A link is not automatic proof. If the text does not contain the answer, the student should recognize that limit and seek another appropriate source. Because generative AI is ultimately a probability engine, running fixed parameters on the context it's given. Human verification remains absolutely essential. Fluent writing simply doesn't equal factual accuracy. So the next time you use an AI tool, ask yourself this. Are you trusting the machine's frozen math or the context you actually provided?
